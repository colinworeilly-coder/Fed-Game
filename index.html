    An independent teaching tool, not affiliated with the Federal Reserve. The model follows the dynamic AD–AS framework in Cowen & Tabarrok, Modern Principles of Economics, chapters 13, 15 and 16 (macro 32, 34, 35).

    Briefing for the new chair

    Keep inflation near 2% and unemployment near its natural rate

    Your tool

    Each quarter you set the federal funds rate, the overnight rate banks charge each other. The Fed hits it with open market operations. Lower rates speed up money and spending growth (M̃ + ṽ) and shift aggregate demand right. Higher rates shift it left.

    Your goals

    Inflation of 2% and unemployment at its natural rate of 4.5%. Your term is 16 quarters. At the end, the President decides whether to reappoint you based on how close you stayed to both goals.

    The catch

    Rate changes take a few quarters to work through spending. Somewhere in your term the economy will be hit by a shock. You won't be told what it is; read the news and the data. Some shocks shift AD, others shift LRAS.

    Real rates matter

    Spending responds to the real rate: the funds rate minus expected inflation. If inflation expectations rise and you leave the funds rate alone, policy gets looser. The neutral funds rate is about 4% when expected inflation is 2%.

    Under the hood

    A quarterly version of the Cowen–Tabarrok AD–AS model

    The game uses the dynamic AD–AS diagram from Modern Principles: real GDP growth on the horizontal axis, inflation on the vertical. All rates are percent per year.

    Aggregate demandπ + g = S

    Spending growth S = M̃ + ṽ must be split between inflation π and real growth g. AD is a line with slope −1.

    What sets spending growthS = E(π) + ḡ − α·m + γ·(u−1 − u*) + d

    With the real rate at its neutral level, spending grows at expected inflation plus normal growth. A real rate above neutral lowers S (AD shifts left). d collects demand shocks: confidence, wealth, taxes, government spending, exports. The γ term is the economy's slow self-correction.

    Policy lagm = (1−ω)·m−1 + ω·(i − E(π) − r*)

    The real-rate gap reaches spending gradually. About half of a rate change bites in the first quarter.

    Long-run aggregate supplyg* = ḡ + z

    The Solow growth rate. Real shocks z (oil, technology, taxes, pandemics) move LRAS.

    Short-run aggregate supplyπ = E(π) + κ·(g − g*) + c

    Upward sloping because wages and prices are sticky. Each SRAS curve is drawn for a given expected inflation rate; c captures cost shocks.

    ExpectationsE(π)+1 = (1−a)·[E(π) + λ·(π − E(π))] + a·2%

    People update expectations toward actual inflation. A credible Fed (high a) keeps them anchored near 2%. Credibility erodes when inflation stays far from target.

    Okun's lawΔu = −β·(g − ḡ − θ·z) / 4

    Unemployment rises when growth falls short of its normal pace. Part of a real shock changes output per worker rather than jobs.
